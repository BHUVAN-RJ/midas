## Problem Statement

The founder wants several personal apps (reminders, calendar, later private photo sharing) and can build them, but does not, because on iOS "it's so fucking annoying to build, deploy, and maintain them." Every prompt-to-app tool publishes to the public internet; none publishes "to me and four friends." Every personal app also needs its own login, and nobody keeps them alive across iOS updates.

### The two theses, stated by the founder 2026-10-01

**Thesis 1: building a one-off app should be easier than searching for one.** This is the bar
the product is measured against, and it is a comparison against search, not against Replit or
Lovable or Claude Code. Today, when a person needs a thing done once, they open a search engine
and go looking for software that already exists, settle for the closest match, and tolerate it.
The claim is that asking for the exact thing and getting it should cost less effort than that
search costs. It replaces every framing that measures this product against a developer's build
time, because a developer's build time was never the thing the user was choosing between.

**Thesis 2, stated as a motto: make everything customizable.** Personalized small software is
the easy half and is not where the need is largest. The need is largest inside big software,
the giants everybody is stuck with, where a user wants one thing changed and has no path to it.
The direction, no mechanism chosen yet: give the makers of large products an SDK that opens
their product to customization from the core down, so a user can run their own build of it,
still in sync with upstream, shaped the way they want. Not a fork that rots, and not a skin on
top. This is the company's direction, not v1 and not v2 scope. A first sketch of the pipeline is
recorded under "the variant build pipeline, discussed 2026-10-01" in the v1 Re-plan section.

Source for thesis 2, read 2026-10-01: Andy Matuschak, "Apps and programming: two accidental
tyrannies" (andymatuschak.org/tat). His point, taken personally: small software is easy to make
and personalize, and big software is where personalization is actually needed. He is on the
against list, and this is the premise revision he produced.

Thesis, in the founder's words: "if building and deploying an app is as easy as a Google search, then why the fuck wouldn't people do something like that?"

Mid-session pivot, recorded: jess is not the deploy layer with voice as an input. jess is an executive assistant, just for the founder, that owns and drives her own apps. Reminders and calendar are her hands. App-building on request is milestone 2. Owning the apps is the point: Siri can only touch Apple Reminders through what Apple exposes; jess has full read/write over apps she hosts.

## Demand Evidence

Honest state: founder is user #1, and the only user with behavioral evidence.

- Founder: "I fucking need this the most." Has a backlog of personal apps not built because of deploy friction. That non-building is the costly workaround.
- Four friends said Apple Reminders and Google Calendar are "horrible." None paid, switched, or built a workaround. Complaint, not demand.
- Amog (friend, named): "really needs to get his life in order." Founder's diagnosis, not Amog's words. Not a user until he asks.
- Sidemen (YouTube) use single-purpose sites for challenges. Evidence that disposable apps are buildable, not that anyone wants jess to build theirs.
- Replit: founder tried it; "publish" is public only. Confirms the private-publish gap.

Session verdict: demand tier B (complaints), self-reported as C. Design is a founder-as-user experiment.

## Status Quo

- Founder: tolerates Apple Reminders and Google Calendar; does not build the apps he wants.
- Friends: low-tech workarounds (Notes, self-texts), no detail given.
- Prompt-to-app tools (Lovable, Bolt, Replit, v0, Base44): web-first, public publish, per-app login, ship-and-forget. Founder has not used Lovable, Bolt, or Base44 seriously.
- "Home-cooked software" discourse (Sloan 2020, Appleton, Robinson, Waxman) names the same blocker: not code, but "terminal, database, deployment pipelines."

## Target User & Narrowest Wedge

- User #1: the founder. A developer who uses Wispr Flow daily and wants that feel for asking an assistant to do things.
- Wedge: jess handles the founder's reminders and calendar, by text or voice, inside a shell app he controls. Two apps, one user, zero App Store friction per app.
- Not v1: Amog, non-coders, sharing, app generation.

## Constraints

- Solo builder. Existing stack: TypeScript, Hono, SQLite (from the Usual repo). Reuse for the mini-app server.
- App Store guideline 4.7: mini apps inside a host app must be HTML5/JS. Dynamically loaded native code is not allowed. Native feel comes from the shell's bridge, not from native mini apps.
- AI-generated content inside an app draws extra review scrutiny; keep generation out of the shipped shell until milestone 2.
- One shell app, one signing identity, one TestFlight, one review.
- Budget: hosting plus Apple developer account.

## Landscape Update, 2026-09-24

A research pass one year after the original layer 1 to 3 sources. Three things changed: the thesis now has named investor demand, the private-publish gap partly closed, and the assistant vendors shipped the shell.

### The thesis is now a YC Request for Startups

Pete Koomen, YC partner and Optimizely co-founder, wrote "AI Horseless Carriages" (April 2025, koomen.dev). Its argument is the assistant-layer twin of Robin Sloan's: the problem is not that AI apps lack AI, it is that the developer owns the behavior and the user cannot touch the system prompt. His line: "In most AI apps, System Prompts should be written and maintained by users, not software developers or even domain experts hired by developers. Most AI apps should be agent builders, not agents." This is external support for the mid-session pivot in Premises 2, and his "agent builders, not agents" is milestone 2 stated in someone else's words.

More directly: Koomen authored a Fall 2026 YC Request for Startups called "A Cloud for Small Software." His framing is the kitchen argument almost verbatim. "Using agents to build personal software to solve your own problems is a lot of fun. This is 'Small Software'," and the blocker he names is deployment, with incumbent clouds built for scale imposing complexity that small software does not need, plus sharing that should be as easy as Google Docs. The same RFS list carries "Self-Maintaining APIs" by Harsha Gaddipati, which is milestone 4 (a maintenance agent that scans a codebase on a breaking change and opens a PR).

Read honestly, this cuts both ways. The kitchen is no longer an unclaimed insight; YC is actively recruiting founders to build it, which means competition is coming and the window for a differentiated wedge is narrower than the original Layer 3 note assumed.

### Personal software discourse, 2026

- Adam Waxman, "Software for One" (July 28, 2026): five apps built for himself, sleep, fitness, marathon, jazz practice, medical records, on Next.js plus Vercel plus Neon plus Better Auth, with Claude Code. Maintenance report: "fixing bugs, adding new features and dependency bumps to be easier than ever. 9/10 times a feedback screenshot shared with Claude gets the job done." The sleep app is shared with his wife and nanny, which is the closest published instance of "publish to me and three people."
- Fabien Girardin, "Software Gets Personal" (Medium, Próximo, January 2026 onward): a multi-part series mapping personal software as a practice and a maker culture rather than a product category.
- Alex Kirk, "Personal Software Needs a Home" (May 14, 2026): names the exact gap this design targets, "Where does this personal software live?", and calls the current state self-hosting chaos. His answer is WordPress as a personal app platform (my.wordpress.net with an app launcher). Same diagnosis as Midas, opposite architecture: a web CMS host instead of a phone-native shell.
- Ink and Switch, "Malleable software" (Litt, Horowitz, van Hardenberg, Matthews, June 2025): the research-side manifesto. Notably it argues AI coding alone is not sufficient to produce personal software.
- Andy Matuschak, "Apps and programming: two accidental tyrannies" (March 3, 2026): the sharpest skeptical read. He grants that non-programmers now build dashboards and automations with agents but calls them peripheral, and argues the unlock is composable architectures plus agents rather than agents generating apps from zero. He is also ambivalent about the result: easy prototyping produces "fragmentary, unfocused working patterns."
- Jeremy Morrell, "Extensible Software in the age of LLMs": "LLMs are really quite excellent at building Software for One. Personal apps that side-step all of the complexity and accountability of enterprise software."

### Evidence against, worth holding

- The Hacker News thread on "Software for One" (item 49096605, August 2026) was broadly enthusiastic but produced the useful objections: that tool-building can be procrastination ("I don't feel like what is missing from my life is I don't have enough tools"), that hosting bills near 2000 dollars a year are absurd for single-user apps when a 2 dollar per month VPS plus SQLite suffices, and that without tests and version control, agent edits silently break working features.
- Security is the strongest counter-evidence. Roughly 62 percent of AI-built applications are reported to ship with critical vulnerabilities, 74 CVEs had been traced to AI coding tools by March 2026, and a May 2026 scan of over 5000 publicly deployed vibe-coded apps found around 40 percent exposing sensitive data. Base44 itself had a critical authentication flaw (Wiz, July 2025) that exposed private applications, patched within 24 hours. For Midas this argues for the single-author, on-device, no-public-URL posture in the Distribution Plan, and against the milestone 2 generator shipping anything network-facing without a review step.
- Matuschak's peripheral-use point is the honest counterweight to the founder-as-user experiment: the people building personal software today are overwhelmingly programmers, which matches the doc's own tier B demand reading.

### Competitor movement since the design

- Replit shipped Private Publishing to Core and Starter plans on May 6, 2026, with External Access Tokens for trusted services. It works at the network level and blocks unauthorized requests before they reach the app. This weakens, but does not close, the Status Quo claim that "publish is public only." It still does not deliver an app to a named friend's phone, and the founder's original Replit finding should be re-tested against this build during the Assignment.
- Lovable added internal-only publishing limited to authenticated workspace members on Business and Enterprise plans.
- Base44 was acquired by Wix for 80 million dollars and positions itself for internal tools and personal projects.
- On-device mini app builders now exist on the App Store: MiniApps Store (Zoomerang), Build Minis, Easyapp. MiniApps Store generates single-file HTML apps from a prompt and runs them sandboxed, but publishes to a public community feed and has no assistant that drives the apps and no private sharing. Closest shipped thing to the shell, and it validates the form factor while leaving both Midas differentiators open.
- Google shipped Opal into the Gemini app (December 2025 onward): natural-language mini apps, Google hosts them, shareable without deploying anything. This is the kitchen, built by a platform, for workflow-shaped apps.

### The biggest change: MCP Apps

On January 26, 2026, the MCP core maintainers, with Anthropic and OpenAI, shipped MCP Apps (extension io.modelcontextprotocol/ui), the first official MCP extension. Servers ship interactive HTML interfaces that the host renders in a sandboxed iframe; tools declare UI templates ahead of time so hosts can prefetch, cache and security-review them; UI and host talk over JSON-RPC and hosts can require explicit approval for UI-initiated tool calls. Supported in Claude web and desktop, Goose, VS Code Insiders and ChatGPT. Figma, Asana and Slack shipped in 48 hours.

This is jess's architecture, shipped by the assistant vendors: an assistant that owns HTML mini apps and can call their actions, with a declared manifest and a permission boundary. The differences that still favor this design are real but need to be defended explicitly:

1. Distribution is directory-first. The published material describes app directories in Claude and ChatGPT as the new app store, and does not describe an unlisted or personal-only path, though a local stdio server inherits the user's own machine permissions and is a plausible personal route on desktop.
2. No phone. MCP Apps live in an assistant's chat surface. Local notifications, alarms, on-device SQLite and offline operation, which are the entire reminders use case, are not addressed.
3. No persistent background execution or notification capability is described in the spec.

The strategic read: jess's defensible claim narrows from "an assistant that owns her apps" to "an assistant that owns her apps on the phone, with notifications, offline, and no directory." If MCP Apps gains a mobile host with local notification access, the moat is the shell and nothing else.

### Platform facts to correct in this doc

- Apple's Mini Apps Partner Program (announced November 13, 2025) is not available to Midas: participating apps get reduced commission (15 percent, moving to 12 percent as of March 15, 2026) on qualifying In-App Purchases, require the Declared Age Range API and Advanced Commerce API, and the program states the mini apps must not be controlled by the host app developer. Midas's mini apps are authored by the host developer and there is no IAP in v1, so the program is irrelevant. Guideline 4.7 remains the operative permission, unchanged. The program does add a Mini App Express Review Channel with a 3 to 5 business day cycle, which matters only if jess later hosts third-party apps at milestone 3.
- Siri AI shipped in the iOS 27 developer builds on June 8, 2026, with personal context, world knowledge and onscreen awareness, roughly 18 months after the original demo. Premise 3's "jess replaces Siri" reasoning still holds, since Siri still reaches apps only through App Intents the app exposes, but the gap is narrower than it was in September 2026 planning and this should be re-checked before milestone 3.
- Android, for the record: the same gap exists but is milder, because sideloading and real PWAs cover much of "just for me." That is closing. Google now requires developer verification for apps installed outside Play on certified devices, enforced from September 30, 2026 in Brazil, Indonesia, Singapore and Thailand, with global expansion announced for 2027. A Hacker News commenter on the Waxman thread named this as the core blocker independently: "Apple essentially blocks sideloading" and "Google is moving there as well."

## MCP Apps, In Plain Terms

What it is, mechanically. MCP is the protocol an assistant uses to reach a tool: the assistant asks a server "what can you do," the server answers with a list of typed actions, and the assistant calls them. Until January 2026 the answer coming back was text or JSON. MCP Apps (extension io.modelcontextprotocol/ui, spec dated 2026-01-26) added a second thing a server can return: a piece of interactive HTML that the assistant renders inline, in a sandboxed iframe, which can talk back to the assistant over the same JSON-RPC channel.

Concretely, one MCP App is three pieces:
1. A server that exposes actions (add_reminder, list_today, reschedule).
2. A UI template, declared ahead of time under a ui:// URI, so the host can prefetch it, cache it, and security-review it before anything runs.
3. A host (Claude, ChatGPT, Goose, VS Code) that renders the UI, keeps the model in the loop, and can require explicit approval before a UI-initiated tool call executes.

Now map that onto the Midas design, piece by piece:

| Midas | MCP Apps |
| --- | --- |
| Mini app, HTML5/JS, served by Hono | UI resource under ui://, served by an MCP server |
| Action manifest the assistant can call | MCP tool list returned by the server |
| Bridge, typed SDK between WebView and shell | JSON-RPC between iframe and host |
| Confirm before cross-app action | Host requires explicit approval for UI-initiated tool calls |
| SwiftUI shell | Claude or ChatGPT, on desktop and web |
| WKWebView per mini app | Sandboxed iframe per app |

That is the same architecture, one to one. The design's central bet, an assistant that owns HTML apps and drives them through a declared manifest, was independently shipped by Anthropic and OpenAI together, six months before this doc was written, and Figma, Asana and Slack were on it within 48 hours.

That is not a reason to stop, but it changes what the design is allowed to claim. The honest framing is now:

- What is no longer differentiated: assistant plus HTML mini apps plus declared actions plus permission prompt. That is a published open standard with two 100M-user hosts behind it.
- What is still differentiated: the phone, and it is a wider gap than the architectural overlap suggests. MCP Apps live inside a chat turn. Nothing in the spec addresses local notifications, alarms, background execution, on-device storage, offline operation, a home screen icon, or launching an app without starting a conversation. A reminder that cannot fire a notification at 7am is not a reminder.
- Distribution. The MCP Apps pitch is directory-first, with the app directories in Claude and ChatGPT described as the new app store. There is no documented unlisted or personal-only publishing path, though a local stdio server inherits the user's machine permissions and is a plausible personal route on desktop. "Nothing to list, nothing to review, nothing public" stays a Midas property. Corrected 2026-10-03: that claim is outdated on web and desktop. A Claude custom connector added by URL is private and unlisted, and Lovable and Base44 now give every published app its own MCP server with OAuth sign-in, usable from Claude and ChatGPT. Private, unlisted apps an assistant can use exist today; what does not exist is a private app with a home on the phone. See the 2026-10-03 landscape update.

Two strategic consequences. First, the bridge SDK in this design should be built so that a mini app's action manifest can be expressed as MCP tools with near-zero translation, because the cheapest future for these apps is to also run inside Claude and ChatGPT on the desktop while the shell owns the phone. Second, the risk to watch is not a competitor, it is a spec update: if MCP Apps gains a mobile host with local notification access, the only thing left is the shell itself, and the founder should know that before spending five weeks.

### Field correction, from daily use

The founder uses Claude every day and pushed back on the assessment above. The pushback is correct and is recorded here because it changes the threat level, not just the tone.

MCP Apps are not a persistent app. They are a UI rendered inside a chat turn. You cannot install one, launch one from a home screen, or be notified by one. Claude cannot today produce a calendar app the founder would run every day, because the artifact or app surface has no app lifecycle: no icon, no background, no alarm, no offline, no place to live outside the conversation that created it. The overlap identified above is real at the architecture level and close to irrelevant at the product level.

One precision in the other direction, for accuracy: MCP Apps are not static pages. The iframe can call server-side tools over JSON-RPC, so an MCP App can read and write a real database. The limit is lifecycle and placement, not capability.

Revised threat level: MCP Apps are not a competitor to this design. They are a precedent. The specific thing to watch is a host on a phone with local notification access. Until that exists, the architectural convergence is a reason to keep the manifest MCP-shaped for cheap desktop reach, and nothing more.

### Field correction, ChatGPT Scheduled Tasks

Also overstated earlier. Scheduled Tasks is a scheduled prompt that delivers a push notification. It is built for recurring work, for example a morning job search digest. It is not a reminders app: there is no reminder object the user owns, no list, no edit, no restructuring of recurrence, no data model. Treating it as a competing reminders app is the grandma-with-wheels error. The week-0 control is still worth running, but as a test the design expects to pass.

## Landscape Update, 2026-10-03: Connectors and Agent-Operated Builders

Question asked: when a person builds their own apps, is there a one-click way to make them
usable from Claude, ChatGPT or any other assistant, and where does that leave this design.
Read from vendor documentation and press, not from hands-on use.

### What exists

- **Per-app MCP servers from the builders.** Lovable ("agent integrations") and Base44 ("App
  MCP") generate an MCP server for each published app, with OAuth sign-in so each user reaches
  only their own data. The owner picks which tools or tables are exposed and pastes the link
  into Claude or ChatGPT. A few clicks, no extra hosting fee. Lovable states there is no
  built-in rate limit or spending cap.
- **Builders operated by agents.** Replit, Lovable and Base44 each expose the whole platform as
  an MCP server, so a user inside Claude or ChatGPT can ask for an app and have it built and
  published without leaving the chat. Replit added this in August 2026. Base44 also ships a
  remote development skill that gives an outside coding agent a sandbox on a Base44 app (read
  and edit files, run commands, preview, checkpoints), and Superagents (March 11, 2026),
  always-on agents reachable on WhatsApp and Telegram. Base44's founder, Maor Shlomo, stated
  the direction publicly: agents, not only humans, are the users, and the product plugs into
  whatever agent the customer already works with.
- **Composio.** A middle layer between agents and existing apps: a catalog of more than 1,500
  apps, managed OAuth per user, and one MCP endpoint with a router that picks tools per
  request. 29 million dollars raised, Series A led by Lightspeed. It accepts custom MCP servers

### What none of them do

- **The phone.** Base44 can produce an App Store build, but it is a web view wrapper submitted
  per app with the owner's own Apple credentials, and Base44's documentation states no push
  notifications and no full offline mode. Lovable and Replit build web apps; their mobile apps
  are for building, not for running what was built.
- **Apps that know about each other.** Each Base44 app is an independent project with its own
  data and permissions. The workaround is copying API code between apps by hand, or letting an
  outside assistant bridge them over MCP. Among the builders nothing matches the context graph
  direction. Corrected 2026-10-04: Wabi claims exactly this, see the Wabi competitor record.
- **Upkeep.** Every product above builds or exposes an app. None maintains it.

### Where it lands on this design

A Sidekick is also HTML in a web view, the same technology as Base44's wrapper. The difference
is packaging: one shell reviewed once, every Sidekick inside it, notifications owned by the
shell, offline from the op log. Exposing an app to an outside assistant over MCP is now a
checkbox feature of the builders and a funded category of its own, so it is not a place to
differentiate. It confirms the 2026-09-24 revision: the assistant is an interface anyone can
supply, and the value is build, private deploy on the phone, one store per person, and upkeep.

Replit operated from inside ChatGPT or Claude is the closest existing form of thesis 1, asking
instead of searching, and it competes with the assistant surface in build 1. That is a framing
question for the Nov 2 application, not a scope question.

### Decisions

1. Build 1 and build 2 scope are unchanged.
2. MCP exposure of Sidekicks, and reaching the generator from an outside assistant, are down the
   line, not v1 and not v2. Both need the manifest action layer removed from SDK v0, the same
   prerequisite as Siri exposure. One platform endpoint covering all of a person's Sidekicks is
   the natural shape, since identity and the store are already per person.
3. Composio is a possible supplier, not a competitor, for the day a Sidekick needs to reach an
   outside service such as Gmail or Google Calendar. Not before Nov 2.
4. Guideline 2.5.2 is added to Accepted risks.

## Survival Path: Consumer First, Enterprise Optional

The question raised after the RFS reading: Koomen's "A Cloud for Small Software" reads like an enterprise pitch, but the founder does not have an enterprise problem and wants to solve this for consumers. Is there a path?

### What the RFS actually says, and what it drifts into

It opens personal: "Using agents to build personal software to solve your own problems is a lot of fun. This is 'Small Software'," and "Purpose-built tools that will only ever have one or a small handful of users." Then within the same entry it drifts to work: "Small software is useful for teams too. Every team does things differently, and there's unlimited demand for bespoke tools," the named hard parts are "every company will want to customize the environment this software runs in, auth & permissions are hard, and allowing nontechnical users to share arbitrary code is tricky to do securely," and the success image is "Small software should be as easy to share with your colleagues as a Google Doc."

Read plainly: the RFS starts at one person and lands at colleagues. That is not an enterprise-only brief, it is the standard bottom-up shape written down. The wedge is personal, the revenue is teams. That is permission for the consumer path, not an argument against it.

### On the architecture reading

The founder's read is correct and worth recording. Azure and AWS are built for software that scales to many users, and most of their complexity is the machinery of that scale: multi-tenancy, regions, autoscaling, IAM designed for org charts, load balancing, quotas. Software with one user or five never touches any of it and still pays the full complexity tax. A cloud for small software can delete most of that, because the workload is small, bounded and purpose-built for a few named people.

The correction, which matters for Midas directly: what is deleted is scale. What is not deleted, and in fact becomes the whole problem, is identity, permissions, sandboxing arbitrary user-authored code, and abuse. Koomen says exactly this in his own list of hard parts. Glitch is the cautionary proof: Fastly shut down Glitch project hosting on July 8, 2025, and the stated reasons were the cost of running millions of apps, abuse by bad actors, and aging architecture. Free hosting for everyone's small apps is the version of this business that dies.

For this design that is reassuring, not threatening. v1 sidesteps all four: one author, one user, on-device data, no public URL, nothing arbitrary being run. The founder inherits the hard part only at milestone 3, and should treat milestone 3 sharing as the moment the real business question opens.

### Comparable companies, as evidence

The founder's Notion instinct is the right pattern. Notion chose to "focus on users, not CIOs" and counted on individuals to carry it into their companies; teams formed around individual accounts, then organizations upgraded. Slack, Figma and Dropbox ran the same motion: individual signup, team formation, IT notices, finance receives an invoice, and the buying decision arrives after the value is already proven. Slack, Figma and Notion all ran net revenue retention above 130 percent in their growth phases, which is what the expansion looks like when it works.

The closest structural analog to a cloud for small software is Tailscale, and it is worth studying in detail because the shape matches almost exactly: a genuinely useful free personal tier (up to 6 users, unlimited devices, not a trial), paid plans starting at 8 dollars per user per month and rising to 18 for organizational control and auditability, growth by word of mouth, and roughly 30,000 businesses paying. Personal use is the product and the funnel at once. It is also solving a neighboring problem, letting a few named people reach private things, which is the same sentence as "publish to me and four friends."

The infrastructure ladder is the same story in a different sector. Heroku, Vercel and Netlify all begin with a hobby tier for personal projects and end at enterprise contracts, with Vercel's enterprise floor around 2,000 dollars per month. The hobby tier is not charity, it is the top of the funnel.

The counter-examples matter too. Obsidian stays consumer, is free for personal use, and monetizes through premium services and a commercial license, which is a real and sustainable business but a small one. Home Assistant is consumer and nonprofit. Both prove a consumer-only personal software company can live; neither proves it can become large.

### The answer on the survival path

Do not go enterprise now, and do not choose now. The v1 in this doc is unaffected: founder-as-user, 30-day kill switch, two apps, no sharing. Nothing in the competitive landscape changes that experiment.

What does need to be decided, and early, because it is a design decision rather than a sales decision: milestone 3 sharing should be built as "invite a named person into an app I own," with per-app permission scoping and an identity that is not tied to a company directory. That sentence is identical whether the named person is a friend or a colleague. Build it that way and the team motion stays available later without a rewrite. Build sharing as a friends-only social graph and the door closes.

Sequenced, the survival path is:
1. One user, the founder, no money, 30 days. This doc.
2. Friends, invite-based, still free, milestone 3. Demand test, not revenue test.
3. If and only if a small group that works together starts using it, the Tailscale ladder becomes available: personal stays free or cheap, small groups pay, larger groups pay for control and auditability.
4. Enterprise is a consequence of step 3, never a starting position, and it arrives through the same door Notion used, a single team already using it.

The thing that kills this business is not failing to sell to enterprises. It is free hosting for strangers' arbitrary apps, which is how Glitch died.

## Revision, 2026-09-24: From Assistant to Infrastructure

Second recorded pivot, after the mid-session pivot of 2026-09-18. The founder's position as of this session:

> "It is going away from just being an assistant to just being this app builder that builds and maintains your apps, whatever apps you need."

That is a different product than the one in Premises 2, and it is a better-defended one. The assistant is now the interface. The product is the thing underneath: generate an app, deploy it privately, keep it alive, let the assistant drive it.

### What this changes

1. **The competitive set moves off the model vendors' roadmap.** As an assistant that handles reminders and calendar, this design competed with the assistant product surfaces of Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and Apple, all of which ship features in that space for free. As infrastructure that builds, deploys and maintains private apps, it competes with Replit, Lovable, Vercel, Base44 and whoever answers Koomen's Fall 2026 RFS. That is a harder competitive set but a fairer fight: those are companies that can be out-positioned, not a vendor that ships your feature as a footnote in a release note.
2. **Milestone 2 is no longer milestone 2.** Generation was gated behind milestone 1 passing 30 days. If generation plus upkeep is the product, that gate is wrong. This needs a re-plan: the founder still needs one app he uses daily to prove the shell, but the thing being proven is now "an app I asked for, that got built, deployed, and kept working," not "an assistant answered my reminder."
3. **Milestone 4, the maintenance agent, moves from last to central.** "Builds and maintains" is the differentiated half. Every competitor listed above builds. None of them maintains. That is also exactly the Fall 2026 RFS "Self-Maintaining APIs" thesis, and the reason the founder's original problem statement said "build, deploy, and maintain."
4. **Success criteria need rewriting.** The 30-day kill switch measures whether the founder stopped opening Apple Reminders. That is the assistant metric. The infrastructure metric is different: how many apps did Midas build, how many are still working 30 days later without the founder touching code, and how many required intervention.

### Premise revisions

- **Premise 2 revised again.** The assistant is the interface, not the product. The product is build plus private deploy plus upkeep. Owning the apps still gives full read and write that no third-party assistant has, and that reasoning is unchanged.
- **Premise on data location, corrected.** The Distribution Plan's "reminders and calendar data live on device" is too strict as a general rule. It is correct where it is sufficient, for example reminders and other small single-user apps, and it stops being possible the moment an app needs sync across devices or more than one participant. A chat app is a backend app by definition. Revised rule: local-first where local is sufficient, backend database where sync or multiple people are required, and the choice is made per app rather than as a platform-wide constraint. Multi-device sync was listed as deferred and not an open question for v1; that deferral now needs a decision, because sync is the difference between a demo and a thing the founder trusts.
- **New premise: this is a paid product from the start.** Generation costs tokens, private hosting costs money per app, and a maintenance agent that runs nightly test suites per app is the most expensive component of all. Free is not on the table. This is also a defense, not just a cost recovery: Glitch died running millions of free apps for strangers under abuse pressure, and priced access removes that failure mode before it starts.

### Consequences to work through

- **Unit economics become a gate, not a footnote.** The existing week-1 gate measures per-day LLM and transcription cost for the assistant. It now needs a second number: the fully loaded monthly cost of one live mini app (hosting, database, the maintenance agent's test runs) and therefore the price floor per app or per user. If a user with 10 small apps costs more than they would plausibly pay, the design changes before code is written.
- **Security moves from a milestone 2 concern to a core design constraint.** Once apps have backend databases and are generated rather than hand-written, the 2026 evidence applies directly: roughly 62 percent of AI-built applications shipping critical vulnerabilities, 74 CVEs traced to AI coding tools by March 2026, around 40 percent of 5000 scanned deployed vibe-coded apps exposing sensitive data, and Base44's own authentication flaw exposing private apps. A platform whose pitch is "I keep your apps alive" is also promising "I keep them from leaking," and that promise is the product.
- **Bridge permission scoping is no longer deferrable.** It was listed under Open Questions for milestones 2 and 3. Generated apps with backend access make it a v1-adjacent requirement.
- **The kitchen framing gets sharper.** Stove is codegen. Kitchen is deploy plus auth plus upkeep. The insight recorded in Layer 3 now has a named investor thesis behind it (Koomen's "A Cloud for Small Software") and a named graveyard (Glitch). The founder's differentiator inside that thesis is the phone: everyone else's small-software cloud ends at a URL, and this one ends at an app on a device, with notifications, that nobody can delist.

### Roadmap risk, recorded

The test applied: is this in the path of the model vendors' own roadmap?

- On the path, and conceded: assistant-driven HTML apps with declared actions (MCP Apps, shipped January 26, 2026), scheduled notifications from an assistant (ChatGPT Scheduled Tasks), personal context and connectors (Anthropic personal connectors April 2026, Siri AI in iOS 27 June 2026), and one-shot app generation (Claude artifacts, Gemini Opal).
- Off the path, structurally: apps that live on the user's device with an icon and a notification, a private deployment with no directory and no listing, data the user owns rather than data inside a vendor account, and an agent whose job is to keep a specific person's app working across platform changes for years. None of these serve a model vendor's interest. Directories, accounts and retention do.
- The asymmetry worth stating plainly: better models make the generation and maintenance halves cheaper and better, so model progress is a tailwind on this design. The only steamroller surface is the assistant chat product, which is precisely the half this revision demotes to an interface.

## Infrastructure: How the Complexity Actually Gets Deleted

Open question raised 2026-09-25: does "a cloud for small software" mean building a cloud from the ground up, or wrapping existing providers the way Vercel wraps AWS?

Answer: wrap, and be the control plane. Owning metal adds cost and operations and differentiates nothing. The product is the deletion of complexity, not the hardware. Revisit only if a provider's per-tenant pricing breaks unit economics at scale, which is a year-three problem.

But the more important finding is that "wrap a provider" is not itself the answer, because the thing that makes small software expensive is not the provider. It is per-app isolation. Big clouds give every app its own container, its own deploy and its own process, which is correct at 100 apps with real traffic and fatal at 10,000 apps with one user each, nearly all idle. Glitch died exactly here: a container per project, millions of projects, almost all dormant, cost scaling with app count rather than with usage.

The rule that follows: an app is a row, not a deployment. Cost per idle app must approach zero.

Five architectural consequences:

1. An app is a static bundle plus an action manifest plus a data namespace. No per-app build server, no per-app container. Generation emits a single-file bundle served from object storage behind a CDN.
2. The runtime is a shared pool of isolates, not containers. App server logic loads on demand in a V8 isolate, milliseconds to start, thousands per machine. Cloudflare Workers is this shape off the shelf; Fly.io or a rented box with a custom isolate host is the same idea if the platform later wants to own it.
3. Data is one small database per app, on a system designed for thousands of them. D1, Turso or libSQL, or Postgres with a schema per app. Never one database instance per app.
4. Identity is a platform primitive, solved once, and no app ever implements login. This directly dissolves the founder's original complaint that every personal app needs its own login.
5. Notifications are centralized. One APNs connection owned by the platform, with apps requesting notifications through the manifest. This is also the phone moat, and it is cheapest as a shared service.

### The constraint that makes upkeep possible

Recorded as the session's eureka. The reason nobody maintains personal apps is dependency and platform drift: npm churn, framework majors, annual OS releases. If the app surface is constrained to HTML plus the platform SDK, with no arbitrary third-party dependencies, drift stops being N separate problems and becomes one problem owned by the platform. Maintaining every user's apps collapses into maintaining one SDK plus automated migrations that are tested once and applied everywhere.

That is the difference between a maintenance agent as a feature and a maintenance agent as a business. It is also hard for competitors to copy: Replit, Lovable and Base44 all sell "any stack you want," which is precisely what makes their output unmaintainable. The constraint is the moat.

Consequence for the bridge SDK: it is no longer just an iOS bridge, it is the app contract, and the README criterion in Success Criteria becomes load-bearing rather than nice to have.

### Data model

The namespace key is the share-group id. Fork creates a new group with one member and a
copied bundle. Shared adds a member to the sender's existing group, where all members
are equal, with no roles and no partial access.

### Storage decision: op-log, offline native

Every write is an operation with a client-generated id, written to local SQLite first so
the UI is instant online or off, sent in the background, deduped server side so retries
are free, and applied last-write-wins per key. Conflicts do not need resolving: new
items get their own keys and never collide, and mutable flags such as "ordered" are
idempotent. No merge algorithm, no conflict UI.

This reverses the earlier instinct to ship online-only. Natural daily use happens in
lifts and on trains, so an online-only app produces no usage evidence. Roughly 2 CC
days, and cheaper now than retrofitting, which would mean rewriting every SDK call site.
Accepted limitation: the store is eventually consistent, so no app may ask "is this true
right now" and expect a strong answer.

### SDK v0: nine calls, and notifications without an API

Revised 2026-09-26 after reading guideline 4.7's sub-rules properly. The notify family is
gone: store.get, store.set, store.query, store.delete, store.claim, user.me, share.members,
share.mode, time.now. The share calls land in build 2, the rest in build 1.

Under the hood every call is a plain HTTPS fetch against the platform backend carrying a
short-lived per-app token. It is not a call into native code. That is deliberate, see the
transport reversal below.

Notifications work with no API at all. A mini app writes an ordinary record,
store.set("reminder:<id>", { at, title, body }), where reminder: is a reserved key prefix.
The native shell watches for those records and schedules the local notification itself, and
the server sends push to other members of a shared group when the record appears. The mini
app has no notification capability, asks for no permission, and cannot fire anything on its
own. Notification authorization belongs to the shell's bundle, is requested once, and is
never delegated. This is less code than the bridge version, not more.

Query stays deliberately tiny (prefix or a single field equality, no operators, no sort, no
pagination), tombstones are excluded from results, and time.now is display only, never used
for ordering. Icons are an SF Symbol name plus a background color in the manifest.
Deliberately not designed for cross-app calls.

### Transport reversal: fetch, not a native bridge

The plan said bridge-only transport with connect-src none, so that the per-app token never
entered page JS. That is the wrong trade under guideline 4.7.2, which forbids exposing native
platform APIs or technologies to non-embedded software without prior permission from Apple. A
WKWebView message-handler bridge that translates web calls into native ones is the canonical
example of the prohibited pattern, and the boundary is what Safari already gives a normal web
page.

So mini apps talk to the backend over ordinary HTTPS fetch with a short-lived per-app token
issued by the shell, and the native surface shrinks to almost nothing. The cost is that the
token is visible to page JS. It is mitigated by short expiry, shell-side reissue, and
server-side scope the client cannot widen, so a leaked token buys an attacker that app's own
data for a few minutes, which is data the app already had. That is a far smaller price than a
rejection.

There is no published process for requesting 4.7.2 permission and no stated turnaround, which
is the deciding factor: the architecture must not depend on an answer that may never arrive
before Nov 2.

### Contract enforcement, revised

The deploy gate can no longer ban fetch, because fetch is now the transport. Labour divides
as follows:

The generator prompt forbids imports, script tags, frameworks and any network call not made
through the SDK. Guidance, not a gate.

The deploy gate rejects the presence of import, require, script src and WebSocket. Those are
code-loading and long-lived-connection primitives, and a presence check on them is
statically decidable.

The runtime contract does the origin restriction, which is where it belongs, because
fetch(a + b) is not statically decidable: CSP with connect-src pinned to the platform origin
only and script-src pinned to a per-deploy sha256 hash. The browser enforces it.

### Ordering, tombstones, and the pooler bug

Two corrections to the op-log design, both from review:

Ordering is by a **server-assigned sequence**, not by client timestamp, because clocks skew
and time.now() is a client call. Client timestamps are display only. Tombstones are excluded
from query results and compete under the same last-write-wins rule.

More important: idempotent last-write-wins is enough for a flag's value and not enough for
the pooler's actual purpose. Two members both flipping an item to ordered does not tell
anyone who was first, so the real failure, two people buying the same thing, was not
prevented. The fix is one extra primitive: store.claim(key) succeeds for exactly one caller,
decided server side on sequence order, and returns false for everyone after. Without it,
duplicate orders would have to be listed as an accepted limitation rather than a solved
problem.

The manifest's "actions the assistant may call" field is removed from v0. In v1 the assistant
builds and edits apps, it does not drive them; assistant-driven actions are deferred with
cross-app wiring.

### Down the line, not v1 and not v2: the variant build pipeline, discussed 2026-10-01

Information only. It changes no scope, no date and no decision, and it sits under thesis 2. The
precedents below were recalled in conversation and have not been checked against sources, so
verify each one before it is cited anywhere outside this doc.

**The question.** How does a platform maintain builds for millions of personal apps, how do app
stores do it, and once makers of large products integrate the SDK, what does the pipeline look
like.

**App stores do not build.** The developer builds, signs and uploads a finished artifact, and the
store reviews, re-signs and distributes it. Google Play comes closest to a server-side build,
generating per-device split APKs from an uploaded App Bundle, and that is mechanical repackaging
rather than compilation. F-Droid is the counterexample: it builds every app from source and is
known for lagging by days. So no pipeline has to be built from the bottom, and the platforms at
this scale get there by avoiding per-app builds rather than by running millions of them. v1
already has this shape, since an app is a row and a single-file bundle with a deploy gate, and
ordinary CI/CD is needed only for the shell.

**One more failure to study, beside Glitch.** Builder.ai, insolvent in 2025: each customer got a
bespoke codebase maintained largely by people, so maintenance cost grew linearly with customers.
It is the build-side version of the lesson Glitch teaches on the hosting side.

**The pipeline shape for thesis 2, founder's statement.** The large product's own CI builds and
tests. The finished build is pushed to this platform. The platform applies each user's
personal changes to that build and publishes the result. That is consistent with the answer
recorded above, where the large product keeps its code and the platform hosts the modified
builds. Three conditions decide whether it works:

1. What gets pushed cannot be a single minified bundle, because a patch against minified output
   breaks on every release. The SDK has to be a build plugin running in the large product's CI
   that emits separate modules with stable identifiers, plus a typed contract for each seam.
2. Applying a user's changes should mean per-user module overrides resolved at load, not a
   rebuild per user. A variant is a set of replacement modules and an import map pointing at
   them, and every untouched module is shared by all users. Cost then follows the modules a user
   touched, not users multiplied by releases.
3. The durable record of a customization is the user's request in words, not the diff. When
   upstream changes a module the user modified, the change is regenerated from the stored intent
   against the new module. This is the same move as editing in place with the previous bundle as
   context, and it is what makes the variant something other than a fork that rots.

**Two limits that follow.** Customization from the core down reaches the client core only: the
large product's server stays with its maker, so server behaviour can be changed only as far as
its API allows. And pinning expires: if a regeneration fails its checks the user stays on the old
base, but only for as long as the large product's API still supports that base. This is the
practical form of the open sub-question above about what the modified client may do against the
giant's API.

**Testing.** The large product's tests cover the base build only. Each variant needs its own
check on the platform side, seam contract checks plus a smoke test, before it replaces the
user's current one.

**Why not configuration or extension points.** They survive every upstream update, and they are
the skin on top that thesis 2 rules out. They remain the cheap tiers for changes that happen to
fit them. Shopify is the warning for the tier below: merchants who edit theme code lose
automatic theme updates.

**Precedents to study, beside Atlassian Forge.** Salesforce managed packages, where a developer
ships a versioned package, each customer customizes on top through metadata, and upgrades land
without wiping the customizations (Weissman and Bobrowski, "The Force.com Multitenant
Architecture", SIGMOD 2009). Debian patch series and Brave on Chromium, the oldest working form
of downstream changes rebased onto every upstream release. Docusaurus swizzling, where users
replace or wrap internal components and upgrades are known to break them, which makes it the
failure mode to study. Webpack Module Federation and single-spa import map overrides, which are
the load-time module replacement mechanism itself. Google's large-scale change tooling, Rosie
(Potvin and Levenberg, CACM 2016), for migrating many codebases by automated change.

### Down the line, not v1 and not v2: device data connectors and widgets, recorded 2026-10-04

Founder's direction. People have paid for devices such as a WHOOP band, and the data those
devices produce sits inside the maker's app. The goal is that a person holds their own data,
sees all of it, and builds their own Sidekicks on top of it. A further, more distant version:
this platform makes its own simple data capture devices, for example a secure baby monitor,
and the user builds the app for it. Nothing here is scope before Nov 2.

**Connectors to existing devices. Feasible, and the natural first step.** The path is server
side: the backend holds an OAuth connection to the device maker's API, pulls data when the
maker's webhook fires, and writes it into the person's store under a reserved prefix, the same
pattern as the reminder records. A Sidekick reads it with the ordinary store calls. No native
API is involved, so guideline 4.7.2 is not touched; guideline 4.7.3 still requires the user's
consent each time a Sidekick is given that data. Limits, using WHOOP as the example: the API is
free and uses OAuth 2.0; development access is capped at 10 members until WHOOP approves the
app; it returns sleep, recovery, strain, workouts and body measurements, and it does not return
continuous heart rate. So "hold all your data" is bounded by what each maker chooses to expose,
and each maker can revoke access. Terra and Open Wearables aggregate many wearable APIs and are
possible suppliers.

**Raw data over Bluetooth. Not available to a Sidekick.** Web Bluetooth does not exist on iOS
in Safari or in a web view. Reading a band's Bluetooth broadcast needs native CoreBluetooth
code in the shell, with the shell uploading to the store. Whether that counts as exposing a
native technology under 4.7.2 is the open question already recorded in the 2026-10-04 notes.

**Own hardware.** A device with its own Wi-Fi that posts directly to the backend avoids every
Apple question, because the phone is not involved in capture. It is also a second business:
manufacturing, radio certification, and for a baby monitor, safety and a security bar higher
than anything else in this plan. Live video is possible in a web view through WebRTC, but the
v0 contract bans WebSocket and pins network access to the platform origin, so it would need a
platform-owned media service, and an alert while the phone is locked would have to come from
the shell, not the Sidekick.

**Widgets. Possible in a fixed, templated form.** A home screen widget is native SwiftUI
compiled into the shell and cannot render HTML, so a Sidekick cannot draw its own widget. The
shell can ship one configurable widget whose instances each point at a Sidekick and render a
small set of native layouts (a number, a short list, a progress ring, a small chart) from a
reserved record the Sidekick writes, again the reminder-record pattern. iOS limits how often a
widget refreshes. A small widget that opens its Sidekick also works as the per-Sidekick home
screen icon that is otherwise not possible. Estimated at a few days once build 2 is done.

Sources: developer.whoop.com; Terra, "WHOOP API in 2026: Data Access, Permissions, and
Limitations"; caniuse.com/web-bluetooth; Apple WidgetKit documentation.

### Accepted risks

Guideline 4.7 and its sub-rules are the largest single risk in the plan, and earlier revisions
of this doc understated them by citing 4.7's permission while ignoring its conditions. 4.7.2
forbids exposing native platform APIs to non-embedded software without prior permission from
Apple, and a WKWebView message-handler bridge is the canonical prohibited pattern, which is
why transport moved to plain fetch and notifications became shell-owned. There is no published
request process and no stated turnaround for that permission, so the architecture is built not
to need it. 4.7.4 requires an index of the software the app offers with universal links to all
of it, satisfied by a per-user backend registry where each generated app has a universal link
that resolves only for authorized members, which is the sharing mechanism doing double duty.
4.7.1 requires content filtering, reporting, timely responses and blocking, dormant while apps
are private and active the moment users share, so build 2 owes a report button and an
owner-side block. 4.7.3 makes a per-app consent prompt mandatory for any privacy permission
ever shared with a mini app, which is another reason nothing is shared. 4.7.5 wants an age
mechanism, unplanned, answerable by pointing out that apps are generated for and by the
account holder. The Mini Apps Partner Program is not available here, since it requires in-app
purchase and states mini apps must not be controlled by the host app developer. Corrected

this category. It is not. Apple states it is optional, a commission tier only; guideline 4.7 is
the route, and it needs no invitation. There is no full version history behind a generator that can now edit apps,

## Sidekicks: the Product Noun, 2026-09-29

The mini apps are called **Sidekicks**. The assistant stays **jess**. Updated 2026-10-04: the
platform is named **Midas**. The trademark, live product and domain checks run on Oct 2 are in
`docs/week0-checklist.md`; still open there are a device-side App Store name check, a lawyer's
USPTO search, and buying a domain.

The thesis is that the plural noun is worth more than the brand. jess is one assistant, but the
product she delivers is many small apps, each one narrow, each one owned by the person who asked
for it. "jess spun up a Sidekick for your marathon training" is a sentence that explains the
whole architecture without a diagram: the shell hosts them, the assistant drives them, and there
is one of them per problem rather than one app that tries to hold every problem. It also gives
the kitchen argument from Layer 3 a concrete object. The kitchen is the shell, the Sidekicks are
what gets cooked in it, and the thing the incumbents ship is the stove.

Naming the outputs rather than the agent also states the milestone 2 position in a single word.
Koomen's "agent builders, not agents" is the claim that the user should own the behavior. A
product whose vocabulary is plural, per-problem apps commits to that claim; a product named after
a single assistant quietly contradicts it. Sidekicks is therefore load-bearing vocabulary, not
decoration: it should appear in the shell UI, the action manifest documentation, and the Nov 2
application.

Why it is a product noun and not the brand. "Sidekick" as a company or App Store name is
occupied and the costs are concrete rather than aesthetic:

- T-Mobile owns a live registered SIDEKICK mark (Reg. 3017191, filed 2003, registered 2005), and
  the Danger, Inc. handset is still the first association for most US adults over 30.
- Borland Sidekick (1984) was a personal information manager: reminders, calendar, notes. Exactly
  the v1 wedge, which makes the name an ancestor rather than a differentiator.
- The Sidekick browser was acquired by Perplexity in May 2025 and sunset on Aug 3, 2025;
  meetsidekick.com now redirects to comet.perplexity.ai. The name currently points at a dead AI
  product owned by a competitor in the assistant layer.
- Live AI assistants already using it: Jigso Sidekick, monday sidekick, Sidekick AI (support),
  getasidekick.ai, TrendSpider Sidekick, plus App Store entries "Sidekick: AI Chat Bot" and
  "Sidekick Spatial AI".

Two consequences that matter for the plan. App Store search is lost on day one, which is a real
cost given that distribution is one shell app through TestFlight and then the Store. And class 9
and 42 are crowded enough that registration is unlikely, which carries rebrand risk landing
exactly when the a16z Tech Week link and the Nov 2 application want a fixed name.

Using Sidekicks as internal and user-facing product vocabulary needs no registration and carries
none of that risk. The brand decision was made on 2026-10-04: Midas.

sidekick.com and sidekicks.app; and a device-side App Store search to confirm the ranking
